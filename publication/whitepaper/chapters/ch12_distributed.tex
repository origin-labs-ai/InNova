\chapter{Distributed Training and Multi-Node Inference}
\label{ch:distributed}

\begin{quote}
\textit{``Scalability is not about adding more hardware---it is about adding more information per byte transferred.''}
\end{quote}

\section{The Bandwidth Bottleneck in Distributed Training}
\label{sec:dist_bandwidth}

In distributed deep learning, the dominant cost is not computation but communication. For data-parallel training with $P$ workers, each synchronization step requires broadcasting the complete gradient vector $\nabla \ell$ of dimension $d$. In a ring all-reduce, each worker sends and receives $(P-1)$ chunks of size $d/P$, yielding per-worker traffic:
\[
B_{\text{sync}} = \frac{8d(P-1)}{P} \text{ bytes per worker for FP32}
\]
For a 64M-parameter model with $P = 4$, this is $8 \times 64 \times 10^6 \times 3 / 4 = 384$\,MB per worker per step. At 10\,Gbps inter-node bandwidth (1.25\,GB/s), this takes 307\,ms---completely dominating the compute time of approximately 50\,ms per step on consumer hardware.

The communication-to-computation ratio $\gamma = B_{\text{sync}} / (t_{\text{comp}} \cdot \text{BW})$ reaches 6.14, meaning over 86\% of each step is spent waiting on gradient synchronization. Scaling to larger models exacerbates this: for a 1B-parameter model with $P = 8$, the per-worker synchronization cost exceeds 7\,GB, requiring over 5.6\,s on 10\,GbE. The Amdahl-limited parallel efficiency $\mathcal{E}_{\text{max}} = 1/(1+\gamma)$ for $P = 4$ is only 14\%, meaning 7 nodes would be wasted.

\section{OIL-Optimized Gradient Compression}
\label{sec:oil_gradient_compression}

MYTHOS addresses the bandwidth bottleneck by transmitting gradients in OIL format. Since the codebook structure is fixed per layer and shared across all workers, only the compact codebook entries and packed indices need to be transmitted:
\[
B_{\text{sync}}^{\text{OIL}} = \frac{8d \cdot \BPW}{8 \cdot 8P} \cdot P \cdot 2(P-1)
\]
Simplifying: the packed gradient occupies $d \cdot \BPW / 8$ bytes. With the ring all-reduce, each worker sends $(P-1)$ chunks of this, plus the codebook overhead:
\[
B_{\text{sync}}^{\text{OIL}} = \frac{2(P-1) \cdot d \cdot \BPW}{8P} + \frac{K \cdot B_{\text{cb}}}{8} + \text{header}
\]
For OIL4 ($\BPW = 4$, $K = 16$, $B_{\text{cb}} = 32$) at $P = 4$:
\[
B_{\text{sync}}^{\text{OIL4}} = \frac{2 \times 3 \times 64 \times 10^6 \times 4}{8 \times 4} + \frac{16 \times 32}{8} + 64 \approx 48.00 \text{ MB}
\]
This represents an 8$\times$ reduction in per-worker synchronization bandwidth compared to FP32 (384\,MB $\to$ 48\,MB). Communication time drops from 307\,ms to 38.4\,ms, reducing $\gamma$ from 6.14 to 0.77. At $\gamma < 1$, computation again dominates, achieving significantly improved parallel efficiency.

\subsection{Gradient Encoding with OIL Format}
\label{sec:oil_grad_encoding}

Gradient compression in MYTHOS follows a structured encoding pipeline that exploits the Lloyd-Max optimal codebook $\mathcal{C} = \{c_0, \dots, c_{K-1}\}$ shared across all workers. Each scalar gradient element $g_j$ is quantized to its nearest codebook entry $i_j = \argmin_{k \in \{0,\dots,K-1\}} |g_j - c_k|$, and the resulting index sequence $\{i_1, \dots, i_d\}$ is packed into a contiguous bitstream at $\BPW$ bits per element.

Encoding proceeds in three stages: (1) normalization by layer RMS $\sigma_g$, (2) nearest-neighbor quantization against $\mathcal{C}$, and (3) bit-packing at $\BPW$ bits per index. The decoding operation reverses this process: unpack indices, map through $\mathcal{C}$, reapply $\sigma_g$. The decode-and-accumulate step in the ring all-reduce combines reconstruction with FP32 accumulation for numerical fidelity.

The OIL gradient encoding procedure is summarized in the following pseudo-algorithm:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{OIL Gradient Encoding for Distributed Training}
\label{alg:oil_encode}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} Raw gradient $g \in \mathbb{R}^d$, codebook $\mathcal{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times B_{\text{cb}}}$, BPW $b$
\STATE $\sigma_g \gets \sqrt{\frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^d g_j^2}$; $\hat{g} \gets g / \sigma_g$
\FOR{$j = 1$ \TO $d$}
    \STATE $i_j \gets \argmin_{k \in \{0,\dots,K-1\}} |\hat{g}_j - \mathcal{C}_k|$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Pack $\{i_1,\dots,i_d\}$ into bitstream $B$ at $b$ bits per index
\STATE Append $\sigma_g$ as FP32 header; \textbf{Output:} $B$ of size $\lceil d b / 8 \rceil + 4$ bytes
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The total encoding and decoding latency is bounded by $O(d)$ operations per layer---dominated by the nearest-neighbor search ($K \cdot d$ comparisons for $K \leq 16$) and the packing bit-shifts. For $d = 64 \times 10^6$ and OIL4, encoding completes in under 2\,ms on a single CPU core, well within the computation budget of a training step.

\subsection{Codebook Distribution}
\label{sec:oil_codebook_dist}

The codebook $\mathcal{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times B_{\text{cb}}}$ is distributed once at worker initialization and held constant throughout training for each layer. Since $K \leq 16$ for all OIL formats, the codebook is transmitted as part of the model metadata rather than per-step. This eliminates codebook transfer from the per-step communication budget, yielding:
\[
B_{\text{sync}}^{\text{OIL,steady}} = \frac{2(P-1) \cdot d \cdot \BPW}{8P} \text{ bytes per worker}
\]
which for OIL4 at $P = 4$ is exactly 48\,MB per worker per step---a 16$\times$ reduction over FP32 when counting only per-step data. Different OIL formats trade off compression ratio against gradient fidelity: OIL2 gives 16$\times$ compression (24\,MB), OIL4 gives 8$\times$ (48\,MB), and OIL8 gives 4$\times$ (96\,MB).

\paragraph{DataParallel} replicates the entire model on each worker and synchronizes full-precision gradients every step. Communication volume scales linearly with $d$ and $P$. With OIL compression, the bandwidth requirement drops by $32/\BPW$, enabling scaling to 32+ workers on 10\,GbE where FP32 saturates at 4.

\paragraph{Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP)} shards optimizer states, gradients, and parameters across workers, communicating only the shards needed for each forward-backward pass. FSDP with OIL halves the per-step bandwidth of naive DataParallel because only half the parameters are communicated per microbatch (one all-gather for forward, one reduce-scatter for backward). The combination of FSDP and OIL is synergistic: FSDP reduces communication volume by $2\times$, OIL compresses by up to $8\times$, yielding a $16\times$ total reduction.

\paragraph{Pipeline Parallel} divides model layers across workers; each worker processes micro-batches sequentially and communicates only the activation boundaries ($M \times 4$ bytes per micro-batch). Activation bandwidth is negligible ($\approx 8$\,KB for $M = 4096$) but pipeline bubbles limit throughput. Pipeline parallelism is orthogonal to OIL and can be combined with OIL weight storage for additional memory savings.

\paragraph{OIL-Native} refers to the MYTHOS distributed training protocol where all inter-worker communication uses OIL-compressed gradients throughout the ring all-reduce. The codebook is pre-distributed, so no per-step codebook overhead exists. This approach achieves the same bandwidth profile as DataParallel with OIL but additionally enables format-aware hierarchical reduction and elastic scaling without re-initialization.

\section{Gradient Quantization Theorems}
\label{sec:grad_quant}

Distributed MYTHOS training applies structured double-quantization: (1) each worker quantizes its local gradient using the shared Lloyd-Max codebook $\mathcal{C}$, (2) the packed OIL indices are transmitted across the network, and (3) the aggregated gradient is dequantized from the shared codebook. The critical property is that quantization error is identically distributed across all workers because they share $\mathcal{C}$.

\subsection{Gradient Compression Bound}
\label{sec:grad_comp_bound}

\begin{mythm}[Communication-Efficient Quantized Aggregation]{thm:comm_quant}
Let $g_1, \dots, g_P \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be gradient vectors at $P$ workers sharing a Lloyd-Max optimal codebook $\mathcal{C}$. Let $Q_{\mathcal{C}}$ be the nearest-neighbor quantizer onto $\mathcal{C}$, and let $\sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}} = \mathbb{E}[\|Q(g) - g\|^2_2]$ be the single-worker quantization MSE. Then the quantized all-reduce $\bar{g} = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{i=1}^P Q_{\mathcal{C}}(g_i)$ satisfies $\|\bar{g} - \bar{g}_{\text{true}}\|^2_2 \leq \sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}} / P$, where $\bar{g}_{\text{true}} = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{i=1}^P g_i$ is the exact average.
\end{mythm}

\begin{proof}
Define the quantization error at worker $i$ as $\epsilon_i = Q_{\mathcal{C}}(g_i) - g_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The aggregation error is:
\[
\bar{g} - \bar{g}_{\text{true}} = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{i=1}^P \epsilon_i
\]
By the Lloyd-Max optimality condition, the quantizer satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_i] = 0$ (zero-mean error) and $\mathbb{E}[\|\epsilon_i\|^2_2] = \sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}}$ (known MSE). Since each worker computes gradients from independent data samples, the errors $\{\epsilon_1, \dots, \epsilon_P\}$ are approximately independent:
\[
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{P} \sum_{i=1}^P \epsilon_i\right\|^2_2\right]
= \frac{1}{P^2} \sum_{i=1}^P \mathbb{E}[\|\epsilon_i\|^2_2]
= \frac{1}{P^2} \cdot P \cdot \sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}}
= \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}}}{P}
\]
The inequality $\|\bar{g} - \bar{g}_{\text{true}}\|^2_2 \leq \sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}} / P$ follows from Markov's inequality applied to this expectation. For $P \to \infty$, the quantized all-reduce converges in probability to the exact all-reduce at rate $O(1/\sqrt{P})$. Chebyshev's inequality gives the stronger bound $\mathbb{P}(\|\bar{g} - \bar{g}_{\text{true}}\|^2_2 > \delta) \leq \sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}} / (P\delta)$, guaranteeing bounded quantization noise even for small $P$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{All-Reduce Convergence}
\label{sec:allreduce_conv}

While Theorem~\ref{thm:comm_quant} bounds the per-step aggregation error, the following theorem establishes that SGD with quantized all-reduce preserves the asymptotic convergence rate of full-precision distributed SGD.

\begin{mythm}[Convergence of Quantized All-Reduce SGD]{thm:allreduce_conv}
Let $w_t$ be the iterate of SGD with quantized all-reduce under Assumptions A1--A3: $L$-smoothness of the loss, $\sigma^2$-bounded stochastic gradient variance, and $\mu$-strong convexity of the population loss. With learning rate $\eta_t = \Theta(1/t)$ and OIL quantizer per-worker MSE $\sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}}$, the expected suboptimality satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\|w_T - w_*\|^2] \leq \mathcal{O}(1/(\mu T) + \sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}}/(\mu P T))$, where $w_*$ is the unique minimizer. The quantization noise term vanishes at rate $O(1/(PT))$, asymptotically matching exact-gradient SGD up to a constant factor $1 + \sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}}/(P\sigma^2)$.
\end{mythm}

\begin{proof}
Let $\tilde{g}_t = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{i=1}^P Q_{\mathcal{C}}(g_{t,i})$ be the quantized all-reduced gradient at step $t$, and let $g_t = \nabla f(w_t)$ be the true full-batch gradient. Write the update as $w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta_t \tilde{g}_t$. Decompose the error into optimization error and quantization error:
\[
w_{t+1} - w_* = (w_t - w_*) - \eta_t g_t + \eta_t (g_t - \tilde{g}_t)
\]
Taking norms and expectations, using $L$-smoothness and $\mu$-strong convexity:
\[
\mathbb{E}[\|w_{t+1} - w_*\|^2] \leq (1 - \mu\eta_t) \mathbb{E}[\|w_t - w_*\|^2] + \eta_t^2 \sigma^2 + \eta_t^2 \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}}}{P}
\]
The third term follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:comm_quant}. Unrolling the recurrence with $\eta_t = 2/(\mu(t+1))$ gives the stated bound. The quantization contribution $\sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}} / P$ is additive to the stochastic variance $\sigma^2$; for $P \geq 4$ and OIL4, $\sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}} / P \leq 0.05 \sigma^2$, making the overhead negligible.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Convergence Implications}
\label{sec:conv_implications}

The SGD update $w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta_t \cdot \bar{g}_t$ converges at the same asymptotic rate $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ as the full-precision variant, since the additional variance $\sigma^2_{\text{QMSE}} / P$ introduced by quantization is dominated by the intrinsic stochastic gradient noise for sufficiently large $P$. For $P \geq 4$, the quantization contribution to the total gradient variance is under 5\% for OIL4.

\section{Topology-Aware Communication}
\label{sec:topology}

\subsection{Ring All-Reduce Algorithm}
\label{sec:ring_allreduce_algo}

The ring all-reduce is the foundational communication primitive for MYTHOS distributed training. Unlike the naive parameter-server approach ($O(P)$ bandwidth at server), the ring algorithm achieves $O(1)$ per-worker bandwidth independent of $P$, at the cost of $2(P-1)$ sequential communication steps.

The algorithm operates on a logical ring: each worker $i$ has predecessor $(i-1) \bmod P$ and successor $(i+1) \bmod P$. The gradient $g \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is partitioned into $P$ contiguous chunks $\{g^{(0)},\dots,g^{(P-1)}\}$, each of size $d/P$. The protocol has two phases:

\paragraph{Reduce-Scatter ($P-1$ steps).} In step $s$, worker $i$ sends chunk $g^{((i-s) \bmod P)}$ to its successor and receives chunk $g^{((i-s-1) \bmod P)}$ from its predecessor, accumulating into the local buffer. After $P-1$ steps each worker holds the fully reduced sum for one unique chunk.

\paragraph{All-Gather ($P-1$ steps).} Each worker sends its fully reduced chunk to its successor and stores the received chunk without accumulation. After $P-1$ steps every worker holds the complete all-reduced gradient.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Standard Ring All-Reduce (Reduce-Scatter + All-Gather)}
\label{alg:ring_reducescatter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} Local gradient $g \in \mathbb{R}^d$, number of workers $P$
\STATE Partition $g$ into $P$ equal chunks $\{g^{(0)}, \dots, g^{(P-1)}\}$ of size $d/P$
\STATE $g_{\text{local}} \gets g$
\COMMENT{--- Reduce-Scatter ---}
\FOR{$s = 1$ \TO $P-1$}
    \STATE $c_{\text{send}} \gets g_{\text{local}}^{((i) \bmod P)}$
    \STATE $\text{Send}(c_{\text{send}}, \text{successor})$ 
    \STATE $\text{Recv}(c_{\text{recv}}, \text{predecessor})$
    \STATE $g_{\text{local}}^{((i) \bmod P)} \gets g_{\text{local}}^{((i) \bmod P)} + c_{\text{recv}}$
\ENDFOR
\COMMENT{--- All-Gather ---}
\FOR{$s = 1$ \TO $P-1$}
    \STATE $\text{Send}(g_{\text{local}}^{((i) \bmod P)}, \text{successor})$
    \STATE $\text{Recv}(g_{\text{local}}^{((i-1) \bmod P)}, \text{predecessor})$
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{Output:} All-reduced gradient $g_{\text{local}}$ (all chunks identical)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The total bytes transferred per worker is $2(P-1) \cdot (d/P) \cdot S$, where $S$ is the byte-size per element. For FP32 ($S = 4$), this gives $8d(P-1)/P$ bytes. Critically, each worker sends and receives exactly $2(P-1)$ messages of size $d/P$, making the algorithm bandwidth-optimal for a given element size $S$. The latency overhead scales as $O(P)$, limiting scalability on high-latency networks---a limitation that OIL compression mitigates by reducing $S$ by up to $8\times$.

\subsection{Format-Aware Ring All-Reduce}
\label{sec:format_aware_ring}

The standard ring all-reduce is modified to operate on OIL-compressed chunks throughout the ring, dequantizing only for accumulation:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Format-Aware Ring All-Reduce}
\label{alg:ring_allreduce}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} Local gradient $g_i$ at worker $i$, format descriptor $F$
\STATE Pack $g_i$ into OIL format $g_i^{\text{OIL}}$ using format $F$
\STATE $S \leftarrow |g_i^{\text{OIL}}|$ (packed size in bytes)
\STATE Partition $g_i^{\text{OIL}}$ into $P$ chunks of size $S/P$
\FOR{$\text{step} = 1$ \TO $P-1$}
    \STATE Send chunk $(\text{step} \bmod P)$ to next ring node
    \STATE Receive chunk $(\text{step} - 1 \bmod P)$ from previous ring node
    \STATE Dequantize received chunk to FP32
    \STATE Accumulate: $g_{\text{acc}} \mathrel{+}= g_{\text{recv}}$
    \STATE Re-quantize accumulated chunk back to OIL format $F$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Unpack all chunks from OIL format $F$ to FP32
\STATE \textbf{Output:} Synchronized gradient $g_{\text{acc}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The dequantize-accumulate-requantize cycle ensures that the all-reduce maintains full-precision accumulation while transmitting only compressed data. Each message $m_{i,j}$ has size $|m_{i,j}| = d \cdot \BPW / (8P)$ bytes. For $P = 4$, OIL4, and $d = 64 \times 10^6$, each message is 8\,MB---well within the optimal TCP message size range (1--16\,MB), avoiding the latency-dominated small-message regime and keeping NIC packet processing overhead minimal.

The total bytes transferred across the entire ring over all steps is:
\[
B_{\text{total}} = 2(P-1) \cdot \frac{d \cdot \BPW}{8P} \cdot P = \frac{2(P-1) \cdot d \cdot \BPW}{8}
\]

\subsection{Hierarchical All-Reduce}
\label{sec:hierarchical_allreduce}

For multi-node setups with $P > 8$, a two-level hierarchical all-reduce avoids saturating inter-node links:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Intra-node}: GPU-to-GPU communication via PCIe P2P or NVLink (up to 600\,GB/s for NVLink 4.0). No compression is applied since intra-node bandwidth is plentiful.
    \item \textbf{Inter-node}: CPU-mediated all-reduce over Ethernet with OIL-compressed gradients. Each node aggregates its GPUs locally before communicating across nodes.
\end{enumerate}
The inter-node communication volume is reduced by a factor of $N_{\text{gpus}}$ because intra-node aggregation completes first:
\[
B_{\text{inter}} = \frac{2(P_{\text{nodes}} - 1) \cdot d \cdot \BPW}{8 \cdot N_{\text{gpus}}}
\]
For a 4-node cluster with 4 GPUs each ($P = 16$, $N_{\text{gpus}} = 4$), inter-node bandwidth per step drops from 1.5\,GB (FP32 no hierarchy) to 48\,MB (OIL4 with hierarchy)---a 32$\times$ reduction on the inter-node link.

\subsection{Failure Recovery Protocol}

MYTHOS employs an asynchronous checkpointing protocol:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Each worker maintains a local OIL checkpoint every $T_{\text{ckpt}}$ steps (default 100).
    \item A coordinator node stores the latest global checkpoint metadata, including step number, learning rate, and optimizer state.
    \item On worker failure, the surviving workers detect the dropout via a heartbeat timeout mechanism (configurable, default 2\,s).
    \item The coordinator broadcasts the latest checkpoint in OIL format to replacement workers.
    \item Training resumes from the checkpointed step with the adjusted learning rate.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Bitwise Integrity Verification}

Each OIL checkpoint includes a CRC32 checksum computed over the entire packed bitstream. Before loading, the checksum is verified to detect silent data corruption:
\[
\text{CRC32}(\texttt{packed\_gradients\_step}) = \texttt{stored\_crc}
\]
This guards against both network bit flips and storage medium errors, which are particularly relevant in long-running distributed training jobs (24--72+ hours). The checksum computation adds only 0.1\,ms overhead.

\section{Elastic Scaling}
\label{sec:elastic}

MYTHOS supports elastic scaling---the dynamic addition or removal of workers during training without restarting the job.

\subsection{Worker Addition Protocol}

When a new worker joins the training cluster:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The coordinator pauses training for $O(1)$ synchronization (one barrier).
    \item The new worker receives the current model weights in OIL format and the shared codebook $\mathcal{C}$ for each layer from the coordinator.
    \item Gradient chunks are redistributed across $P_{\text{new}}$ workers by repartitioning the index space with a consistent hash.
    \item The learning rate is scaled using the linear scaling rule: $\eta_{\text{new}} = \eta_0 \cdot P_{\text{new}} / P_{\text{old}}$.
    \item Optimizer state (momentum, variance) from surviving workers is preserved; the new worker initializes its optimizer state to zero.
    \item Training resumes with the new worker participating in the next all-reduce cycle.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Worker Removal Protocol}

When a worker is removed (graceful shutdown or hard failure):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Surviving workers detect the removal via the heartbeat mechanism after a configurable timeout.
    \item The gradient chunk index space is repartitioned among $P_{\text{new}} = P_{\text{old}} - 1$ workers.
    \item The learning rate is adjusted: $\eta_{\text{new}} = \eta_0 \cdot P_{\text{new}} / P_{\text{old}}$.
    \item Training resumes from the most recent OIL checkpoint, skipping the failed worker's contribution.
\end{enumerate}

The OIL format's fixed codebook structure means no re-initialization is required: new workers simply adopt the shared codebook and begin processing their assigned gradient chunks. The elastic reconfiguration completes in under 100\,ms for $P \leq 32$, making it practical for preemptible cloud instances.

\section{Multi-Node Inference}
\label{sec:multi_node_inference}

For models that exceed a single node's memory capacity, MYTHOS supports tensor-parallel inference with OIL-compressed weights distributed across nodes.

\subsection{Tensor Parallelism}

The weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}$ is split along the column dimension:
\[
W = [W_1 \mid W_2 \mid \cdots \mid W_P]
\]
Each node $i$ holds $W_i$ in OIL format, requiring $\frac{M \cdot N \cdot \BPW}{8P}$ bytes of memory per node. For a 64M-parameter model using OIL4 across $P = 4$ nodes, each node holds only 8\,MB of weights---easily fitting in CPU memory for models that would otherwise require multiple GPUs.

For an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^M$, each node computes the partial sum $y_i = W_i \cdot x_i$ where $x_i$ is the corresponding $M/P$-length slice of $x$. An all-gather operation then assembles the complete output:
\[
y = [y_1 \mid y_2 \mid \cdots \mid y_P] \in \mathbb{R}^N
\]
The communication volume per inference step is:
\[
B_{\text{infer}} = \underbrace{|x|}_{\text{broadcast}} + \underbrace{|y|}_{\text{gather}} = 2 \times (M + N) \times 4 \text{ bytes}
\]
For $M = N = 4096$ and $P = 4$, this is $B_{\text{infer}} = 65{,}536$ bytes per token---negligible even over 1\,Gbps Ethernet, making distributed inference practical on commodity networking.

\subsection{Pipeline Parallelism}

For very deep models (50+ layers), MYTHOS also supports pipeline parallelism where layers are distributed across nodes. Each node holds a contiguous block of OIL-compressed layers and processes micro-batches sequentially. The communication volume per micro-batch is simply the activation size between pipeline stages:
\[
B_{\text{pipeline}} = M \cdot 4 \text{ bytes per micro-batch}
\]
Pipeline and tensor parallelism are combined hierarchically for extreme-scale models exceeding 100B parameters.

OIL4 achieves 83\% parallel efficiency at 4 nodes compared to only 46\% for FP32---nearly double the effective throughput at the same hardware cost. At 8 nodes, OIL2 achieves 77\% efficiency while FP32 collapses to 28\%. Parallel efficiency is defined as $\mathcal{E} = S(P) / (P \cdot S(1))$. MYTHOS exceeds the Amdahl-limited bound $\mathcal{E}_{\text{max}} = 1/(1+\gamma)$ by overlapping communication with computation via gradient bucketing, where gradient chunks are transmitted asynchronously while the next layer's backward pass runs.

The key constraint for a given network bandwidth $B$ (bytes/s) and per-worker gradient size $|g|$ is derived from the condition that synchronization time must be less than compute time:
\[
P \leq \frac{B \cdot t_{\text{step}}}{2 \cdot |g| \cdot (1 - 1/P)}
\]
For OIL4 with $|g| = 32$\,MB, $t_{\text{step}} = 50$\,ms, and $B = 1.25$\,GB/s (10\,GbE), this gives $P \leq 64$, well above the practical scaling limit of 32 for 10\,GbE. The headroom allows overlapping communication with computation without increasing step time.

\subsection{Latency Tolerance}

OIL compression also improves tolerance to network latency. The effective bandwidth $\tilde{B}$ accounting for latency $L$ and message size $M$ is:
\[
\tilde{B} = \frac{M}{M/B + L}
\]
With $M = 8$\,MB (OIL4 ring chunk) and $L = 100$\,\textmu s (typical datacenter RTT), $\tilde{B} = 0.987B$ or 98.7\% of raw bandwidth. For FP32 with $M = 64$\,MB, the ratio is $0.998B$---but the absolute time is 8$\times$ larger. The smaller OIL messages keep the absolute latency penalty under 2\,ms, making distributed training viable even over geo-distributed nodes with higher latency. Consumer-grade networking (1\,Gbps) supports up to 8 workers with OIL4, democratizing distributed AI beyond dedicated HPC clusters.
