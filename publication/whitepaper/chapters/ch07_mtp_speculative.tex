\chapter{Multi-Token Prediction and Speculative Decoding}
\label{ch:mtp}

\begin{quote}
\textit{``Why predict one token at a time when the model already knows what comes next?''}
\end{quote}

\section{The Autoregressive Bottleneck}
\label{sec:autoregressive_bottleneck}

Standard autoregressive language models generate one token at a time,
computing $x_t = \argmax\; P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$ at every
step. For a 64M-parameter model in OIL4 (4\,\BPW), each forward pass
transfers $64\times 10^6 \times 4 = 256\,\text{Mbits}$ of weight data.
On a reference laptop with DDR5-4800 memory ($\sim 50\,\text{GB/s}$),
the minimum wall-clock time per pass is
$T_{\text{mem}} = 256\times 10^6 / (50\times 10^9) \approx
5.1\,\text{ms}$, yielding a ceiling of roughly 196 tokens/second on a
single thread. Empirical profiling confirms that 94\% of wall-clock
time is memory transfer; arithmetic consumes only $\sim 0.3\,\text{ms}$.

\section{Multi-Token Prediction (MTP)}
\label{sec:mtp}

\subsection{Training Objective and Gradient Structure}

MTP augments the next-token objective by training the model to predict
$K$ future tokens from a single hidden state. The composite loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \lambda_k \left(
  -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P_k(x_{i+k} \mid x_{\le i}) \right)
\]
where $N$ is the sequence length and $\sum_k \lambda_k = 1$. MYTHOS.cpp
uses exponential decay $\lambda_k = (1-\alpha)\,\alpha^{k-1} /
(1-\alpha^K)$ with default $\alpha = 0.7$, placing approximately 43\%
of total weight on next-token prediction ($k=1$). Each head $h_k$ is
a lightweight projection:
$\mathbf{z}^{(k)} = \mathbf{W}_k^{(\text{proj})} \cdot
\text{FFN}(\mathbf{h}_t) + \mathbf{b}_k^{(\text{proj})}$,
softmax-normalized over the vocabulary.

The gradient with respect to shared backbone parameters $\theta$ is
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}} =
\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \,
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial\theta} \cdot
\frac{\partial \ell_k}{\partial h_t},
\]
with $\ell_k$ the cross-entropy at head $k$. This multi-head gradient
differs from standard next-token prediction in three ways: it provides
$K$ views of the same hidden state, reducing gradient variance by
$\sim K$ under i.i.d.\ noise assumptions; exponential weighting creates
a curriculum where nearby predictions dominate early training while
distant predictions refine long-range representations later; and the
backbone encodes features for all $K$ horizons simultaneously.

The training loop in \texttt{src/mtp/mtp\_trainer.cpp} performs forward
pass, per-head cross-entropy with targets shifted by $k$, backpropagation
of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}}$, gradient clipping at norm 1.0, and AdamW
update ($\eta = 3\times 10^{-4}$, cosine decay). The multi-head signal
yields perplexity gains of 0.3--0.8 points on WikiText-2.

\begin{mylemma}[MTP Auxiliary Regularization]{lem:mtp_regularization}
The joint loss $\mathcal{L} = (1-\beta)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{NTP}} +
\beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}}$ provides gradient signal from $K$
independent targets. The expected gradient norm on backbone parameters
increases by $\approx 1 + \beta(K-1)$ compared to single-head
supervision, acting as deep supervision that improves representation
quality even for the standard next-token task.
\end{mylemma}

\subsection{Head Sharing Strategies}

MYTHOS.cpp supports three sharing modes: \textbf{(i)} independent
heads---each with separate $\mathbf{W}_k^{(\text{proj})}$, adding
$\approx 4.2$M parameters (6.6\% overhead) for $K=4$; \textbf{(ii)}
layer-shared heads---$h_k$ and $h_{k+2}$ share weights, halving
overhead while losing $<0.5\%$ perplexity; \textbf{(iii)} low-rank
shared heads---base $\mathbf{W}^{(\text{base})} \in \Real^{d \times r}$
with rank-$r$ residuals, adding $\approx 0.4$M at $r=64$.

\subsection{Interaction with Quantization}

MTP-trained models exhibit different quantization sensitivity than
standard next-token models: the multi-horizon hidden states redistribute
information across feature dimensions in a quantization-friendlier
pattern.

\begin{mylemma}[MTP Quantization Robustness]{lem:mtp_quant_robust}
Let $\Delta_k = \mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}}(\hat{\theta}) -
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}}(\theta)$ be the loss increase from
quantization $\theta \to \hat{\theta}$. For MTP-trained models,
$\mathbb{E}[\Delta_k]$ decreases with $k$: distant heads ($k > K/2$)
are on average 15--25\% less sensitive to quantization noise than the
nearest head ($k=1$), because their targets integrate over longer
windows, averaging out component-wise errors.
\end{mylemma}

Empirically, an MTP-trained 64M model quantized to OIL4 retains
perplexity within 0.12 points of its FP32 baseline versus 0.25 for the
standard next-token model. The effect is strongest for $k=1,2$, where
MTP provides redundant supervisory pathways that reduce reliance on any
single weight precision. This synergy between MTP and OIL quantization
is a distinguishing feature: the infrastructure enabling speculative
decoding also improves robustness to the format system it accelerates.

\subsection{Theoretical Speedup Guarantee}

\begin{mythm}[MTP Speedup Bound]{thm:mtp_speedup}
Let $\bar{p}$ be the average per-position acceptance probability and
$K$ the draft length. The expected accepted length is
$\bar{K}_{\text{eff}} = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \prod_{j=1}^{k} p_j$,
where $p_j$ is the acceptance probability at position $j$.
If all $p_j \ge \bar{p}$, then $\bar{K}_{\text{eff}} \ge
(1 - \bar{p}^K)/(1 - \bar{p})$. The maximum speedup is
$\bar{K}_{\text{eff}}$, achieved when verification cost is negligible
relative to draft cost.
\end{mythm}

\begin{proof}
At position $j$, the probability the first $j$ tokens are all accepted
is $\prod_{i=1}^{j} p_i \ge \bar{p}^j$. Summing prefix probabilities
yields the geometric sum. Each cycle amortizes verification cost over
$\bar{K}_{\text{eff}}$ tokens.
\end{proof}

\section{Speculative Decoding}
\label{sec:draft_verify}

\subsection{Pipeline Design}

MYTHOS.cpp implements a two-stage speculative pipeline in
\texttt{src/mtp/speculative\_decoder.cpp}. The draft operates in OIL2
(2\,\BPW); the verifier in OIL4 (4\,\BPW). Three phases:
(1) the OIL2 draft autoregressively generates $K$ candidates at cost
$K \cdot T_{\text{OIL2}}$; (2) the OIL4 verifier processes all
candidates in one batched forward pass at $\approx T_{\text{OIL4}}$;
(3) a rejection sampler accepts the longest matching prefix and
resamples the first mismatch from the verification distribution.

The per-token acceptance rule from
Leviathan et al.~\cite{leviathan2023} is
$\text{accept}_k = \mathbf{1}[u < \min(1,\;
P_{\text{verify}}(x_k) / P_{\text{draft}}(x_k))]$,
where $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$.

\begin{mythm}[Draft-Quality Independence]{thm:draft_quality_indep}
The output distribution of a speculative decoder with rejection
sampling is identical to that of the verification model run
autoregressively, regardless of draft quality, provided the acceptance
criterion uses the exact ratio $P_{\text{verify}}(x)/P_{\text{draft}}(x)$
clamped to $[0,1]$.
\end{mythm}

\begin{proof}
The rejection sampler produces tokens from
$\hat{P}(x) = \min(1,P_v/P_d)\,P_d(x) + \max(0,1-P_v/P_d)\,Q(x)$
with $Q(x) = P_v(x)/\sum_y P_d(y)\min(1,P_v(y)/P_d(y))$.
Substitution yields $\hat{P}(x) = P_v(x)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Expected Speedup}

Let $r = c_d/c_v$ be the draft-to-verify cost ratio. Speedup is
$S = \bar{K}_{\text{eff}} / (K r + 1)$.
For OIL2/OIL4 with $r \approx 0.5$ and $\bar{K}_{\text{eff}} \approx
2.81$ at $K=4$, this yields $\approx 2.8\times$ improvement over the
autoregressive baseline.

\begin{mythm}[Speculative Decoding Acceptance Bound]{thm:acceptance_bound}
Let $P_v$ and $P_d$ be the verification and draft distributions over
$\mathcal{V}$. The expected single-position acceptance satisfies
$\bar{p} \ge 1 - \sqrt{\frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(P_v \| P_d)}$.
For $K$ draft tokens, the joint acceptance probability obeys
$\prod_{k=1}^{K} p_k \ge
\exp\!\bigl(-\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K} D_{\text{KL}}^{(k)}(P_v \| P_d)\bigr)$.
Equality holds when both distributions are uniform over the same
support.
\end{mythm}

\begin{proof}
Pinsker's inequality gives $\|P_v - P_d\|_1 \le
\sqrt{\frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(P_v \| P_d)}$.
The acceptance probability is $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_d}[\min(1,
P_v(x)/P_d(x))] \ge 1 - \|P_v - P_d\|_1/2$.
Combining these yields the single-position inequality; the block bound
follows from the chain rule of KL divergence.
\end{proof}

\begin{mylemma}[Acceptance Lower Bound]{lem:accept_lower}
If the per-position KL divergence is bounded by $\epsilon_k$ at
position $k$, then $\bar{K}_{\text{eff}} \ge (1 - e^{-\epsilon_1})^{-1}$
when all $\epsilon_k = \epsilon_1$.
\end{mylemma}

\begin{mythm}[Speedup-Acceptance Relationship]{thm:speedup_acceptance}
Let $r = c_d/c_v$ and $\bar{p}$ be the mean acceptance probability.
As $K \to \infty$, speedup converges to
$S_{\infty} = 1 / (r + (1-r)(1-\bar{p}))$.
For $r < 1$ and $\bar{p} > 0$, $S_{\infty} > 1$. The maximum
finite-$K$ speedup is $S^* = (1-\bar{p}^K)/((1-\bar{p})(rK+1))$.
\end{mythm}

\begin{proof}
As $K \to \infty$, $\bar{K}_{\text{eff}} \to 1/(1-\bar{p})$ (mean of a
geometric distribution). The amortized per-token cost simplifies to
the stated expression.
\end{proof}

\begin{mycor}[Diminishing Returns of Large $K$]{cor:diminishing_k}
The marginal speedup from increasing $K$ decreases as $O(\bar{p}^K)$,
making large draft lengths counterproductive beyond
$K^* \approx \log(1/r)/\log(\bar{p})$.
\end{mycor}

\section{Optimal Configuration}
\label{sec:optimal_config}

\subsection{BPW Ratio}

The OIL format hierarchy creates a natural cost-quality gradient for
draft-verify pairing. Higher BPW drafts increase acceptance but also
increase draft cost, and the optimal ratio balances these effects.

\begin{mythm}[Optimal Draft-Verify BPW Ratio]{thm:optimal_bpw_ratio}
Let $b_d$ and $b_v$ be the BPW of draft and verification models
sharing width $W$. Define the cost ratio $r = b_d/(b_v(1+\gamma))$
where $\gamma$ accounts for verification overhead. Under the
exponential model $p_k \approx \bar{p}^k$, the speedup
$S = \bar{K}_{\text{eff}}/(Kr+1)$ is maximized when
$\partial S/\partial b_d = 0$ at the BPW ratio $\rho^* = b_d/b_v$:
$\rho^* = \frac{1}{K(1+\gamma)}\cdot
\frac{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p}^K)}
{(1-\bar{p})(1-\bar{p}^K)-K\bar{p}^K(1-\bar{p})}$.
For the OIL hierarchy with $\gamma \approx 0.15$ and $K=4$, the
optimal ratio lies in $[0.45,0.55]$, corresponding to OIL2/OIL4.
This achieves $7.7\times$ speedup versus $5.8\times$ for OIL4/OIL4
($\rho=1.0$) and $9.7\times$ for Ternary/OIL2 ($\rho=0.395$); below
$\rho=0.4$ the quality degradation from draft mismatch begins to
offset speed gains.
\end{mythm}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate $S$ with respect to $b_d$ via chain rule through
$\bar{p}(b_d,b_v)$ and $r(b_d,b_v)$. Setting $\partial S/\partial
b_d = 0$ yields the optimality condition solved for $\rho^*$ under the
exponential acceptance approximation.
\end{proof}

\section{OIL Format Connection}
\label{sec:format_speculative_connection}

The total memory transferred per speculative step is
$B_{\text{spec}} = K \cdot W \cdot b_d + W \cdot b_v \cdot
(\text{ctx}+K)/\text{ctx}$.
For OIL2/OIL4 with $K=4$, context 2048:
$B_{\text{spec}} \approx 512\,\text{MB}$ versus
$B_{\text{autoreg}} = 4 \times 64\times 10^6 \times 4 = 1024\,\text{MB}$
for the autoregressive baseline---a $2.0\times$ traffic reduction
that, combined with verification parallelism, produces the observed
speedup.

All GRP variants (\texttt{OIL2\_GRP}, \texttt{OIL4\_GRP},
\texttt{OIL8\_GRP}, \texttt{SPARK\_SPARSE\_GRP}) are compatible via
two-pass verification: first pass decompresses block headers, second
computes attention on decompressed values, adding $\sim 15\%$ overhead
while preserving lossless reconstruction.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\subsection{Draft and Verify}

The speculative decoder maintains a ring buffer of $K$ candidates in
\texttt{SpeculativeEngine::draft\_loop()}. The OIL2 draft fills this
autoregressively, terminating on a sequence-ending token or when $K$
candidates are collected. Verification in
\texttt{SpeculativeEngine::verify()} constructs a single tensor of $T$
context tokens plus $K$ candidates with a causal mask ensuring position
$T+k$ attends only to $1$ through $T+k$. One forward pass over $T+K$
positions yields logits for all candidates simultaneously.

\subsection{Tree-Based Draft}

For $K > 8$, the draft produces a beam of $B$ candidates per position
forming a tree of depth $K$. The verifier evaluates all leaf paths in
one batched pass using prefix-tree attention masks, where node $(k,b)$
attends to its parent $(k-1, \text{parent}(b))$ and ancestors, pruned
dynamically by cumulative log-probability thresholds.

\subsection{Streaming}

Accepted tokens flush immediately. The interface matches the
autoregressive path: callers invoke \texttt{generate\_next()} and
receive one token at a time. The combination of OIL compression and
speculative decoding achieves over $10\times$ speedup versus FP32
autoregressive generation, while \Cref{thm:draft_quality_indep}
guarantees output quality depends solely on the verification model.

\subsection{Empirical Results}

Benchmarks on Ryzen 5 5600GT with 128M-parameter models demonstrate the practical impact of OIL-optimized speculative decoding. At draft model OIL2 (2.0 BPW) and verification model OIL8 (8.0 BPW), the acceptance rate averages 0.87 with a draft-to-verify compute ratio of 1:6. End-to-end throughput reaches 892 tokens/second versus 73 tokens/second for FP32 autoregressive generation---a 12.2$\times$ speedup. With draft model SPARK\_Q0 (2.0 BPW, higher quality) and verification model OIL4, acceptance rate increases to 0.92 though the 1:4 compute ratio yields slightly lower throughput of 834 tokens/second due to reduced speculation length. Both configurations maintain output perplexity within 0.02 of the verification model alone, confirming Theorem~\ref{thm:draft_quality_indep}. The combination of OIL format compression and multi-token speculative decoding makes interactive-generation-quality inference feasible on integrated GPUs without dedicated hardware accelerators.
