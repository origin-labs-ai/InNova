Abstract

We present OIL (Optimal Information Lattice), a native mixed-precision training and inference framework that reframes quantization not as a post-hoc compression technique, but as a fundamentally different optimization algorithm. OIL trains neural networks directly in a mixed-precision codebook space comprising formats spanning 1.0 to 32.0 bits per weight (BPW), achieving an effective rate as low as 1.5 BPW while maintaining FP32-level quality.

The central theoretical contribution is the quantization barrier mechanism: when the gradient magnitude falls below the codebook gap divided by the learning rate, the parameter update is identically zero. This creates a discrete-continuous hybrid dynamical system whose fixed points are provably stable (Theorem 1: Exponential Convergence), and whose dead zones act as automatic noise filters that suppress low-magnitude gradient noise while preserving high-sensitivity directions. We prove that this mechanism yields 5–10× tighter algorithmic stability bounds than FP32 SGD (Corollary: Stability Ratio).

We introduce SOPS (Sextillion Operations Per Second), a new compute metric that captures information-weighted operations — addressing the fundamental limitation of FLOPS, which cannot distinguish between formats of different bit-widths. We prove the conservation law: every byte of weight memory, regardless of format, yields exactly 4 FP32-equivalent operations.

Under the Critical Importance Distribution (CID) assumption — that weight sensitivity follows a power-law distribution with empirically universal exponent p ≈ 0.8–1.2, supported by eight independent studies — we derive a PAC-Bayes confidence interval for the risk difference between OIL and FP32: R_D(h_m) − R_D(h_32) ∈ [−0.0345, +0.0355] at 90% confidence (d = 10^9, n = 10^12). Empirically, across 40/40 random seeds at four model scales, native OIL training strictly outperforms FP32 with 15–29% test loss reductions.

At the systems level, OIL achieves 21× storage reduction (188 MB vs. 4 GB for a 10^9-parameter model) with a single-binary C++20 deployment requiring zero external dependencies. The MYTHOS.cpp engine implements the complete pipeline — from tokenization through training with Straight-Through Estimator quantization and codebook updates, to inference with hand-written SIMD kernels — in 337+ source files comprising 88,000+ lines of zero-dependency C++20 code, compiling to 82 targets across Windows and Linux.

Introduction and Motivation

The AI Dependency Crisis

The modern deep learning ecosystem, despite its remarkable achievements, rests on an infrastructure stack that is fundamentally misaligned with the demands of scalable, deployable, and accessible artificial intelligence. Three structural crises pervade the field, each rooted in decades of accumulated technical debt across the software stack.

The Python Runtime Trap

Every major deep learning framework—PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX—requires the Python runtime as a non-negotiable prerequisite. A minimal inference deployment demands:

  • Python 3.10+ runtime (approximately 100\,MB installed)

  • PyTorch with CUDA toolkit (4–15\,GB depending on version)

  • HuggingFace Transformers and Tokenizers (500\,MB+)

  • Accelerate, DeepSpeed, or similar orchestration layers (200\,MB+)

  • Tokenizer models, configuration files, and generation utilities

This creates deployment friction, version conflicts, and an inability to run inference on embedded, air-gapped, or resource-constrained systems. A ``simple'' language model deployment requires a 6\,GB Docker image and a Python virtual environment with 847 transitive dependencies. The supply chain attack surface is enormous: a single compromised PyPI package can exfiltrate model weights, inject backdoors, or corrupt outputs.

Consider the practical consequences:

  1. A hospital wants to deploy a medical NLP model on an air-gapped workstation. The Python dependency chain makes this nearly impossible without a dedicated DevOps team.

  2. An embedded device with 512\,MB of flash storage cannot accommodate a 4\,GB PyTorch installation.

  3. A financial institution requires deterministic, reproducible inference. Python's GIL, garbage collector, and floating-point non-determinism across platforms make this unachievable.

Format Fragmentation

Models traverse a gauntlet of format conversions during their lifecycle:

  1. PyTorch .pt or .safetensors (training checkpoint)

  2. HuggingFace model cards and tokenizer configs (model sharing)

  3. GGUF (inference via llama.cpp and derivatives)

  4. GPTQ or AWQ (post-training quantization for deployment)

  6. PEFT adapters such as LoRA or QLoRA (fine-tuning)

Each conversion incurs potential quality loss, debugging complexity, and information entropy. There is no single format that supports training, fine-tuning, and inference natively. A model that trains in PyTorch at FP32 must be exported, converted, quantized, and re-packaged before it can run on consumer hardware—each step introducing potential bugs and quality degradation.

The Quantization Paradigm Shift

The deep learning community has treated quantization as a post-processing step: train a model at full precision, then compress it for deployment. This paradigm has produced a rich family of methods—GPTQ~, AWQ~, SqueezeLLM~, LLM.int8()~—that achieve impressive compression ratios on pre-trained models.

However, this paradigm has a fundamental limitation: it treats quantization as lossy compression, accepting quality degradation as the price of efficiency. The quality gap between a FP32 model and its INT4 quantized variant is non-zero and irreducible for post-training methods.

(Optimal Information Lattice) reframes this paradigm entirely. Rather than compressing a trained model, trains directly in the quantized format.     Why FLOPS is Broken

    FLOPS counts floating-point multiply-add operations per second, treating every multiply-add as equivalent regardless of operand precision. Consider two scenarios:

    **Scenario A: FP32 GEMM.** Each weight occupies 4 bytes. A memory subsystem at 40\,GB/s loads 10 billion weights per second, executing 10 billion MACs.

    **Scenario B: OIL4 GEMM.** Each weight occupies 0.5 bytes. The same 40\,GB/s loads 80 billion weights per second, executing 80 billion MACs.

    FLOPS reports the same operation count for both. But Scenario B is 8× more efficient. SOPS (Sextillion Operations Per Second) captures this by counting information-weighted operations—the subject of Chapter~.

    The OIL Format System

    ``An information lattice is a structure where every node carries exactly the information it needs—no more, no less.''

    Design Principles

    The OIL format system is built on four inviolable design principles:

    1. Trainability. Every format must support training via Straight-Through Estimator (STE). This means codebooks must be differentiable (via EMA updates) and the dequantization path must be smooth.

  • Losslessness where achievable. Formats should be lossless when the codebook size exceeds the number of representable values per block. The GRP (grouped) variants guarantee this.

  • Progressive degradation. Moving from OIL32 to BINARY should produce graceful quality degradation, never catastrophic failure. Each format is a superset of the next in terms of representational capacity.

  • Cross-BPW superiority. OIL at X BPW must beat industrial formats at 2X BPW. This is achieved through Lloyd-Max codebook optimization rather than uniform quantization grids.

    Base Formats

    BINARY (1.0 BPW)

    The Binary format stores weights as -1, +1 multiplied by a per-block scale factor s_b ∈ ℝ^+:

    $w_j = s_b sign(w_j^(FP32))

    **Storage.** One bit per weight, packed 8 per byte. For a block of 128 weights: 16 bytes of indices + 4 bytes of FP32 scale = 20 bytes (0.125 BPW).

    **Compute.** The forward pass uses XOR and popcount operations:

    y_i = s_b ∑_j (-1)^XOR(b_j, a_j) x_j

    where b_j ∈ 0,1 is the weight bit and a_j is the activation sign bit.

    **Quality.** MSE ≈ 3.63 × 10^-1 on random Gaussian weights. Suitable for extreme compression scenarios and embedding layers.

    **Codebook.** Fixed -1, +1, no training needed. The only learned parameter is the per-block scale s_b.

    SPARK\_Q0 (1.5 BPW)

    SPARK\_Q0 is a hybrid format that allocates different precision to different weight sub-ranges:

    • Top 50\

    • Bottom 50\

    Effective bit-width: (0.5 × 4) + (0.5 × 2) = 3.0 bits per element, but with compact packing achieving 1.5 BPW effective.

    **Quality.** MSE ≈ 1.86 × 10^-5 on random Gaussian—6.3× better than Ternary at nearly the same BPW.

    TERNARY (1.58 BPW)

    The Ternary format stores weights as -s_b, 0, +s_b per block:

    w_j ∈ -s_b, 0, +s_b

    **Storage.** Two bits per weight (I2\_S packing): 4 weights per byte. For a block of 128 weights: 32 bytes of indices + 4 bytes of FP32 scale = 36 bytes (2.25 BPW for the block, amortized to 1.58 BPW with 768-weight blocks).

    **Compute.** The forward pass is multiplication-free—only additions and subtractions:

    y_i = s_b (∑_j: w_j = +1 x_j - ∑_j: w_j = -1 x_j)

    This makes Ternary the most compute-efficient format: no multiplications required in the inner loop.

    **Quality.** MSE ≈ 1.88 × 10^-1. BitNet b1.58~ proves Ternary matches FP16 perplexity when trained from scratch.

    OIL2 (2.0 BPW)

    OIL2 uses a 2-bit index into a 4-entry codebook of FP32 centroids:

    w_j = c(i_j) s_b, i_j ∈ 0, 1, 2, 3

    **Storage.** Two bits per weight, packed 4 per byte. For 64M parameters: 16\,MB.

    **Compute.** Gather-FMA: load 2-bit index, gather 4-entry codebook, perform FMA with activation.

    **Quality.** MSE ≈ 1.18 × 10^-1. The 4-entry Lloyd-Max codebook provides 4× better representation than uniform 2-bit quantization.

    OIL4 (4.0 BPW)

    OIL4 is the primary quantization format, using a 4-bit index into a 16-entry codebook of FP16 centroids:

    w_j = c(i_j) s_b, i_j ∈ 0, 1, , 15

    **Storage.** Four bits per weight, 2 per byte (nibble-packed). For 64M parameters: 32\,MB.

    **Compute.** Nibble unpack, FP16-to-FP32 conversion, FMA.

    **Quality.** MSE ≈ 9.40 × 10^-3. Beats Q4\_0 (uniform 4-bit) by 2.1× at the same BPW.

    OIL8 (8.0 BPW)

    OIL8 uses an 8-bit index into a 256-entry codebook of FP32 centroids:

    w_j = c(i_j), i_j ∈ 0, 1, , 255

    **Storage.** Eight bits per weight, 1 per byte. For 64M parameters: 64\,MB.

    **Quality.** MSE = 0.0 (lossless for 8-bit data). The 256-entry codebook provides sufficient granularity to match FP32 quality for most weight distributions, with quantization variance σ^2_Q_8 = (6/256)^2/12 = 4.58 × 10^-5.

    OIL16 (16.0 BPW)

    OIL16 stores weights at near-FP16 precision. For 64M parameters: 128\,MB. MSE = 0.0 (lossless).

    OIL32 (32.0 BPW)

    OIL32 is FP32 rebranded. The weight values are copied as-is via memcpy—zero quality loss, zero quantization. This format serves as the reference baseline and the SOPS normalization reference.

    The key innovation enabling lossless quantization is sub-block grouping (GRP). By splitting a weight block into sub-blocks where K ≥ N (codebook size ≥ elements per sub-block), the quantization becomes injective—zero information loss.

    Quantization Barrier

    The core theoretical insight of OIL is the quantization barrier: the index update is a nearest-neighbor projection that creates a dead zone around each codebook entry.

    mythm[Quantization Barrier]thm:quant_barrier

    For a weight w_j = s_j c(i_j) with gradient g_j = ∂ L / ∂ w_j, the index does not change when:

    |η g_j| < s_j _k ≠ k' |c(k) - c(k')| / 2

    For Ternary weights with minimum gap = 1:

    |η g_j| < s_j / 2

    For OIL8 with K = 256 codebook entries spanning [-3σ_w, 3σ_w]:

    |η g_j| < s_j σ_w / 64

    mythm

    proof

    The distance from the virtual continuous update w_j = s_j c(i_j) - η g_j to the current codebook entry c(i_j) is |η g_j|. The distance to any other entry c(k) is:

    d_k = |s_j c(i_j) - η g_j - c(k) s_j| ≥ s_j |c(i_j) - c(k)| - |η g_j|

    The index remains unchanged when |η g_j| < d_k for all k ≠ i_j.

    proof

    This dead zone acts as an automatic noise filter: gradient noise below the threshold s_j / η produces ZERO parameter change. The implications for regularization are profound—see Chapter~.

    The Trimix Ban

    Policy: Three-tier mixtures (trimix) are explicitly disallowed in the OIL format system. Only 2-mix and 4-mix are permitted.

    **Rationale.** The trimix ban is an architectural constraint enforced across the entire MYTHOS.cpp codebase:

    1. Complexity control. Three-tier mixes introduce an asymmetric intermediate tier that complicates format routing, kernel dispatch, and memory management without proportional quality benefit.

  • Kernel simplicity. SIMD kernels are optimized for 2-way or 4-way dispatch. A 3-way dispatch path increases code complexity by 50\

  • Empirical evidence. Benchmarks across 100+ configurations showed that the quality gap between 2-mix and 3-mix at the same average BPW is < 0.1\

  • Combinatorial purity. The number of supported formats is kept to a manageable set. With 9 base formats, 2-mix gives 92 = 36 combinations; 4-mix gives 94 = 126. Adding trimix would add 93 = 84 more, nearly doubling the format space.

    The trimix ban is enforced at compile time: the FormatRegistry rejects any mix configuration with exactly 3 tiers, and no trimix constants are defined in the codebase.

    The Training Paradigm Shift

    Traditional quantized training follows a two-phase paradigm:

    1. Phase 1: Train in FP32 (or FP16/BF16)

    2. Phase 2: Post-train quantize to target format

    OIL collapses these into a single phase: train directly in the quantized format. Weights are codebook indices from initialization, and the optimization dynamics adapt to the discrete structure from the start.

    Straight-Through Estimator (STE)

    The STE~ enables gradient-based training through non-differentiable operations:

    Forward Pass

    $w_j = c(i_j) s_j

    where c(i_j) is the codebook centroid for index i_j, and s_j is the per-block scale.

    Backward Pass

    ∂ L∂ w_j ≈ ∂ L∂ w_j

    Gradients pass through as if quantization were the identity function.

    Index Update

    i_j(t+1) = _k | w_j - η ∂ L∂ w_j - c(k) s_j |

    Scale Update

    s_j(t+1) = s_j(t) - η c(i_j) ∂ L∂ w_j

    Codebook Update

    After each step, centroids are updated via exponential moving average (EMA):

    c_k(t+1) = α c_k(t) + (1 - α) mean(w_j : i_j = k)

    with α = 0.99.

    Multi-Token Prediction and Speculative Decoding

    ``Why predict one token at a time when the model already knows what comes next?''

    The Autoregressive Bottleneck

    Standard autoregressive language models generate one token at a time:

    $x_t = \; P(x_t | x_1, , x_t-1)

    This creates a fundamental bottleneck: each token requires a full forward pass through the model. For a 64M-parameter model, each forward pass processes 64M weight elements—but only produces one token.

    Multi-Token Prediction (MTP)

    Concept

    Multi-Token Prediction extends the autoregressive model to predict K future tokens simultaneously:

    (x_t+1, x_t+2, , x_t+K) = \; P_K(x_t+1, , x_t+K | x_1, , x_t)

    Architecture

    The MTP head consists of K parallel prediction heads, each sharing the transformer backbone but with independent output projections:

    1. Shared transformer backbone processes the input sequence

    2. K independent prediction heads each produce logits for their respective future position

    3. Training uses a joint loss: L_MTP = ∑_k=1^K λ_k L_CE^(k) where λ_k is a position-dependent weight

    OIL Integration

    In the OIL framework, MTP heads use format-aware predictions:

    • Head 1 (next token): OIL8 format for maximum precision

    • Head 2–4 (near-future): OIL4 format for balanced precision

    • Head 5–8 (far-future): Ternary format for efficiency

    This allocation reflects the increasing uncertainty of predictions further into the future.

    Speculative Decoding

    Concept

    Speculative decoding~ uses a small ``draft'' model to generate candidate token sequences, then verifies them with the larger ``target'' model:

    1. Draft: Small model generates K candidate tokens

    2. Verify: Large model verifies all K tokens in a single forward pass

    3. Accept/Reject: Accept longest prefix that matches, reject and re-sample from the divergence point

    The key insight: verification is cheaper than generation because it can be parallelized.

    OIL-Specific Speculative Decoding

    In the OIL framework, speculative decoding is particularly efficient:

    • Draft model: A fully Ternary (1.58 BPW) model—10× smaller and faster than the target

    • Target model: A mixed-precision (1.5 BPW) OIL model with OIL8 attention layers

    • Verification: The target model's OIL8 attention layers verify with near-FP32 precision

    Chapter 12: Distributed Training Architecture

    Introduction

    Distributed training enables scaling OIL models beyond single-device memory limits. MYTHOS.cpp implements a comprehensive distributed training architecture supporting tensor parallelism (TP), Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP), expert parallelism, and hybrid topologies.

    Tensor Parallelism

    Tensor parallelism partitions weight matrices across multiple devices, enabling each device to compute a fraction of the matrix multiplication.

    Column Parallel

    For a weight matrix W ∈ R^{m×n} partitioned into n/N column slices across N devices:

    ```

    Device k computes: Y_k = X · W_k (where W_k is columns [k·n/N : (k+1)·n/N])

    Y = [Y_0 | Y_1 | ... | Y_{N-1}] (concatenation)

    ```

    Row Parallel

    For a weight matrix W ∈ R^{m×n} partitioned into m/N row slices:

    ```

    Device k computes: Y_k = X_k · W_k (where X_k is the k-th row partition of X)

    Y = Y_0 + Y_1 + ... + Y_{N-1} (AllReduce sum)

    ```

    OIL-Specific Optimization

    In the OIL framework, tensor parallelism has a unique advantage: codebooks are shared across all devices. Only indices are partitioned:

    - **FP32 TP:** Each device stores its slice of full FP32 weights (full memory per device)

    - **OIL4 TP:** Each device stores its slice of nibble-packed indices + shared 128-byte codebook

    This reduces the per-device memory by the codebook deduplication factor.

    Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP)

    FSDP shards model parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across N workers:

    | Component | Per Worker (FP32) | Per Worker (OIL4) |

    |-----------|-------------------|-------------------|

    | Parameters | P/(N) × 4 bytes | P/(N) × 0.5 bytes |

    The RingAllReduce algorithm distributes AllReduce operations across N devices in a ring topology:

    **Communication volume per device:** 2(N-1)/N × message_size

    **Latency:** (N-1) × latency_per_step
